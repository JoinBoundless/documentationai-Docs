---
title: The removal of a rival and the dissolution of a republic
---
## **The man who wept over Carthage**

In the spring of 146 BC, Scipio Aemilianus stood inside the burning ruins of Carthage and wept. The city had held out for three years. Its people had fought from the rooftops of buildings that were already on fire. When the end came, the Romans killed or enslaved the survivors and levelled the city stone by stone. The commander who finished the work was a Roman general at the height of his career, and he was crying.

He turned to the Greek historian standing beside him and grasped his hand. Polybius recorded what he said. Scipio was thinking of Troy, and he spoke the line from Homer:

> "A day will come when sacred Troy shall perish, And Priam and his people shall be slain."
> \- Polybius, Histories 38.21, quoting Homer, Iliad 6.448-449

Polybius asked him what he meant, and Scipio answered that he was thinking of the fickleness of fortune, and that some day the same doom would be pronounced on his own country. The account survives as a fragment, preserved by later writers. Appian repeats it in his history of the Punic wars, and so does Diodorus Siculus.

The general who destroyed Rome's greatest enemy stood in the ashes and understood that Rome would end the same way. He named a mechanism, and he left the date open.

## **What the Romans believed held them together**

Sallust wrote his history of the Jugurthine War around 41 BC, three years after Caesar was killed. He looked back at the Republic the way a man looks back at a house that has already burned down. He asked what had once kept the Romans decent, and he gave an answer that has never been improved on.

> "For before the destruction of Carthage the people and senate of Rome together governed the republic peacefully and with moderation. There was no strife among the citizens either for glory or for power; fear of the enemy preserved the good morals of the state."
> \- Sallust, Jugurthine War 41

The Latin for that last clause is *metus hostilis*, the fear of the enemy. Sallust treated it as the thing that had been holding the whole structure up. He made the same argument a year earlier, in the Catiline's War of 42 BC, where he writes that once Carthage had perished root and branch and all seas and lands lay open, fortune began to grow cruel. The exact phrase belongs to the Jugurthine War. The argument runs through both. In the fragmentary Histories he extended it, observing that the Republic had been at its best between the second and the final Carthaginian wars, and that when the threat from Carthage had been removed, the Romans were free to resume their quarrels.

Seventy years later, a Roman officer and historian named Velleius Paterculus put the same verdict in one sentence:

> "For, when Rome was freed of the fear of Carthage, and her rival in empire was out of her way, the path of virtue was abandoned for that of corruption, not gradually, but in headlong course."
> \- Velleius Paterculus, Roman History 2.1

What Sallust is describing is an incentive. He is not praising a national character, and he is not blaming one. The fear of an enemy was doing work that the Romans themselves never had to do, and when the enemy went away, the work stopped.

## **The senator who argued for keeping the enemy alive**

Rome had a man who saw all of this in advance.

Cato the Elder ended every speech in the Senate by repeating that Carthage had to be destroyed. That much is reported by four ancient authors, Plutarch, Pliny the Elder, Florus and Aurelius Victor. One detail is worth knowing before anyone quotes him in a modern essay. No ancient source records those words in Latin. Plutarch, who wrote in Greek, gives Cato's formula as *δοκεῖ δέ μοι καὶ Καρχηδόνα μὴ εἶναι*, Carthage must not exist. The famous Latin version, *Carthago delenda est*, was assembled by English and French scholars at the end of the eighteenth century, and German scholars built the longer form, *ceterum censeo Carthaginem esse delendam*. The slogan is a modern artefact standing in for a phrase the ancients never wrote down in that language.

Cato's opponent was Publius Cornelius Scipio Nasica Corculum, consul in 155 BC and censor in 159 BC. Nasica answered him in the Senate with the opposite declaration, and Plutarch preserves both formulas:

> "In my opinion, Carthage must be destroyed."
> \- Plutarch, Life of Cato the Elder 27, the formula Cato repeated at the end of every speech
>
> "In my opinion, Carthage must be spared."
> \- Plutarch, Life of Cato the Elder 27, the formula Nasica used in reply

Plutarch gives Nasica's reasoning with it. Nasica wished the fear of Carthage to remain, so that it would curb the boldness of the crowd like a bridle on a horse. He judged Carthage strong enough to be taken seriously and weak enough to be survived. Diodorus Siculus reports the same debate, and the summaries of Livy's lost books record it as well. Historians treat the substance of the exchange as historical and treat the wording as Plutarch's reconstruction of it. A related caution for anyone quoting the other side: the English phrase "let Carthage exist" is a modern paraphrase with no ancient source behind it.

Nasica lost.

## **What followed Carthage**

In the same year that Carthage fell, Rome sacked Corinth and dissolved the Achaean League. The consul Lucius Mummius took the city, and Greece became a province. For a moment Rome had no rival left anywhere in the Mediterranean.

Here is what the next seventy years look like, in order.

In 133 BC, the tribune Tiberius Gracchus proposed to redistribute public land to the dispossessed. A mob of senators beat him to death in the street, led by the pontifex maximus, who was his own cousin.

In 121 BC, his brother Gaius Gracchus, tribune for two years running, was killed on the Aventine hill along with thousands of his followers. The Senate had just passed the first final decree in Roman history, the *senatus consultum ultimum*, which authorised the consuls to use any force necessary to save the state.

In 88 BC, the consul Sulla marched a Roman army on Rome. It was the first time in four centuries that the city had been taken by its own soldiers. Marius was declared an outlaw.

In 87 BC, Marius came back with Cinna and killed the men who had supported Sulla.

In 82 and 81 BC, Sulla won the civil war and posted lists of the condemned. The list was the instrument, and it worked by price. Appian records the first posting at about forty senators and sixteen hundred knights, with more names added in the days that followed, a bounty paid for each head delivered, and sons of the proscribed barred from public office. Appian's own summary of what the city had become is blunt: no unseemly deed was left undone until Sulla had made himself sole master of the state.

From 82 to 79 BC, Sulla ruled as dictator under a title that named his job, *legibus scribundis et rei publicae constituendae*, for writing laws and settling the state. He cut the powers of the tribunes, shored up the Senate, and then did something no tyrant had done before. He resigned, and died the next year in his bed.

Seventy years separate the burning of Carthage from the point where Roman politics had become a sequence of proscription lists. The Republic still had its magistrates and its assemblies, and it had stopped being able to hold itself together.

## **The two factions**

Romans gave the two sides names. The populares were the men of the people. The optimates were the best men. The words name political styles and postures, and they describe no organisation a modern reader would recognise. There was no membership, no platform, no manifesto and no whip.

Nineteenth century historians, Mommsen chief among them, read the two labels as rival parties. That reading is now considered anachronistic. Badian, Astin, Gruen and Mary Beard all treat them as loose and shifting alignments and as weapons of political language. The populares included the Gracchi, Marius, Cinna and later Caesar and Clodius. The optimates counted the Scipiones, the Metelli, Cato the Younger, and Cicero himself in his own account of himself.

The correction matters, because it sharpens the argument. Two organised parties with rules and a referee are a way of containing conflict. Loose factions fighting over the same prize, with nobody standing outside the fight, have no such restraint. While the enemy was Carthage, the leading men of Rome had somewhere to point when they needed the city to close ranks. With Carthage gone, the only prize left was Rome itself.

The value of Rome to its inhabitants fell, exactly to the degree that the two sides tore each other apart. Neither side would have pushed as far as it did if it had needed the other to fend off a common enemy. The moment the pressure came off, they fought. The moment they fought, they lost the thing they were fighting over.

## **The end of the Republic**

The Republic dissolved by degrees into the hands of one man.

In 27 BC, Octavian made a settlement with the Senate. He gave back the emergency powers he held, and received in return a province, the loyalty of the armies stationed in it, and a name. The Senate called him Augustus. He called himself *princeps*, first citizen, and the arrangement is known as the Principate. The substance was one man's command of the legions for as long as he lived.

Tacitus, writing more than a century later, opened his history at the moment it happened:

> "After the conflict at Actium, and when it became essential to peace, that all power should be centered in one man, these great intellects passed away."
> \- Tacitus, Histories 1.1

In his Annals he says that Augustus took the whole state under his empire under the title of prince.

The Romans had one word for the thing they had spent nearly five hundred years refusing. They called it *regnum*, kingship, and they treated the accusation of it as a licence to kill. They had thrown out the Tarquins for it. Brutus and Cassius had killed Caesar for it. So the men who ended the Republic were careful never to use the word. They kept the magistracies, the forms and the titles, and the reality underneath changed completely. Sallust had the language for it a generation earlier, and his words are the ones in the dictation that started this essay. A people who lose their own republic become slaves to a king again, and the last thing they lose is the vocabulary for it.

## **The principle**

Political scientists have a name for what Sallust described, and it was written down properly only in the twentieth century.

The conflict cohesion hypothesis holds that conflict with an outside group increases cooperation and solidarity inside a group. Lewis Coser set it out in 1956 in The Functions of Social Conflict. A group under external pressure tightens. A group without that pressure has to find some other reason to hold together, and often finds none. Muzafer Sherif demonstrated the mechanism at Robbers Cave between 1949 and 1954, where boys at a summer camp formed tight in-groups the moment they were placed in competition with another group for scarce prizes, and turned on each other when the competition was removed. The finding behind both is that competition over scarce resources raises in-group solidarity. Take the competing group away, and the solidarity goes with it.

One distinction is worth stating precisely, because it is easy to get backwards. Diversionary war theory predicts the opposite direction of causation. It holds that leaders who face division at home start a foreign quarrel in order to rally support and quiet the opposition. That is the theory behind the phrase rally round the flag, and it runs the other way. Conflict cohesion and diversionary war are opposites. Sallust described the first one. The men in this essay who started foreign wars were rich, and they had nowhere left to point.

## **The same shape, four hundred years earlier**

Greece had already run the experiment.

In 480 BC the Persian fleet was destroyed at Salamis. In 479 BC the Persian army was beaten at Plataea and left Greece for good. In 478 or 477 BC the Greek cities formed the Delian League, with Athens at its head, to finish the war and keep Persia out of the Aegean. For a generation the alliance had a purpose every member could see.

Then the purpose receded. Athens converted the league into an empire. Thucydides records the mechanism in a single sentence. The allies, he writes, no longer fought as being on an equality, and the tribute was turned into subjection by force.

In 431 BC the Greek cities went to war with each other, and the war lasted twenty seven years. Thucydides wrote the coldest passage in ancient literature about what it did to them, describing the civil war at Corcyra:

> "Words had to change their ordinary meaning and to take that which was now given them."
> \- Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War 3.82

He lists what replaced the old meanings. Reckless audacity became the courage of a loyal ally, and prudent hesitation became specious cowardice. In the same history the Athenians tell the Melians that the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must. That is the sound a system makes when nothing outside it is holding it in.

## **The same shape, an ocean and a continent away**

The American founders built the fear of a rival into the machine on purpose.

James Madison, writing Federalist No. 51 in 1788, described the design in two sentences that are quoted often and read rarely:

> "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition. The interest of the man must be connected with the constitutional rights of the place."
> \- James Madison, Federalist No. 51

Then he gives the reason, which is a statement about human nature and not about law:

> "If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary."
> \- James Madison, Federalist No. 51

Madison's claim is that ambition is the material the constitution is built from. Set the interest of one office against the interest of another, and each one keeps watch on the other. He calls it supplying, by opposite and rival interests, the defect of better motives, and says the policy runs through the whole system of human affairs, private as well as public.

This is the constructive half of the argument. Rome's problem after 146 BC was structural. The machine had nothing inside it that had to hold, because the pressure outside it was gone.

## **Where the pressure is gone today**

Modern cases are harder to use, and honesty requires saying which ones fit and which ones do not.

The lysine cartel of 1992 to 1995 fits the mechanism partially. Five producers, led by Archer Daniels Midland, agreed on prices for an animal feed additive with no substitutes. The average delivered price in the United States went from 68 cents a pound to a plateau of 98 cents by late 1992, and stayed there while the members met in hotel rooms to allocate volumes. This is the withholding of a rival in the plainest sense, and the public paid the difference. Archer Daniels Midland pleaded guilty in 1996 and paid a 100 million dollar fine, then the largest criminal antitrust fine in American history, and three executives went to prison. The case belongs in a paragraph about competition withheld, and the honest note is that it involves no external enemy.

The Chicago meatpacking story does not fit the pattern at all. The Beef Trust was concentration, which is a different mechanism. Swift and Company lost its case at the Supreme Court in 1905. Upton Sinclair published The Jungle in 1906, and the Pure Food and Drug Act and the Meat Inspection Act followed that same year. The structural remedy came later, in 1921, with the Packers and Stockyards Act. One candidate case that does not fit, stated plainly, is worth more than three that only appear to.

One tempting parallel fails the evidence, and it is worth naming the failure. The claim that the United States fragmented internally after its rival bloc disappeared in 1991 does not survive the scholarship. Yugoslavia is the defensible case. With the bipolar structure gone, the federal centre lost the ability to mediate between rival republics, and the country came apart in war from 1991. Historians argue about this one, and it should be presented as an argument.

The mechanism has been documented, named and running for a very long time. Every system that has ever held together has held together for a reason. When the reason is a rival outside the walls, then the removal of that rival is the removal of the reason. A Roman could be greedy at the expense of the state without endangering the state, because after 146 BC there was nobody left who was able to make the state fail.

The only way to keep a system good is to create enough internal opposition that it has to cohere to survive.
