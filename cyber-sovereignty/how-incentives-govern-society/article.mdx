## **The schoolboy in Nagasaki**

For the schoolboy in Nagasaki on August 9, 1945, the question of American intent held no meaning. Whether Truman acted from strategic calculation, moral conviction, or cruelty changed nothing about the rubble where a school had stood. History may long debate whether the bombing was justified. The child beneath it had no such luxury. The harm was done. The motive was irrelevant to the outcome.

Societies spend extraordinary energy evaluating the moral character of those who govern them. One asks: are they good people? Do they mean well? Are they honest? These questions feel vital. Something else determined the outcome in Nagasaki, and it determines the outcome everywhere else too.

## **Incentives produce behavior**

The world operates on incentives. One goes to work in the morning to feed a family. A child attends school to avoid consequences. A first job is taken because money is needed. In each case the behavior produced is identical regardless of moral reasoning. Most people do not face an open field of choices. One who does not go to work faces serious consequences, and the fear of the consequences drives them. The incentive presented creates the behavior.

The engine underneath is simple. People move toward pleasure and away from pain. Psychologists call this the hedonic principle, and it runs deeper than ideology or character. A person will do almost anything to avoid pain and almost anything to gain pleasure. Incentives work because they attach pleasure and pain to specific behaviors.

> "Show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome."
>
> \- Charlie Munger, 1995 Harvard speech

Consider the evidence:

1. **Wells Fargo (2002-2016):** Goodhart's law states that when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. A sales quota is meant to stand in for something real: customers who need accounts. Wells Fargo made the quota the target. Employees opened millions of fraudulent accounts to meet it, and 5,300 of them were fired for fraud. The measure stopped measuring what it was supposed to, and the incentive structure rewarded the fraud, so the fraud happened.

2. **The cobra effect:** In colonial India, the British government wanted fewer cobras in Delhi, so it offered a bounty for every dead cobra brought in. The plan backfired. People began breeding cobras to collect the bounty. When the government discovered the scheme and cancelled the reward, the breeders released their cobras into the streets. The city ended up with more cobras than it started with. The British wanted one thing, and the incentive they built produced the exact opposite.

3. **The decline of war:** For most of human history, raiding and conquest paid. A stronger tribe could take land, livestock, and people, and the spoils were worth the risk. Then trade developed, and buying became cheaper than conquering. A trading partner is worth more alive than dead, because a dead partner cannot produce goods to trade. War destroys the very wealth one would otherwise trade for. Steven Pinker documents the result: violence declined dramatically over centuries. No one elected more peaceful leaders. The economics of raiding simply became worse than the economics of buying. The incentive changed, and the behavior followed.

The incentive produced the behavior every time.

## **Why moral framing misleads in politics**

In the political realm, the belief that society runs on moral choices leads to one specific, repeated failure: the search for honest leaders.

> "If men were angels, no government would be necessary."
>
> \- James Madison, Federalist No. 51 (1788)

Madison understood this. Yet modern democracies still operate as though electing the right person solves the structural problem. Gilens and Page (2014, Princeton) found that "the preferences of the average American appear to have only a minuscule, near-zero, statistically non-significant impact upon public policy." Economic elites and organized business groups hold substantial independent influence. The system's incentives reward serving funders over constituents, so every occupant of the system serves funders over constituents.

> "It is difficult to get a man to understand something when his salary depends upon his not understanding it."
>
> \- Upton Sinclair

Consider national debt. Each new government borrows freely, playing hot potato with the bill and hoping the opposing party will inherit it. The incentive is to spend, appear prosperous, and pass the consequences forward. US national debt now exceeds $40 trillion. No party that accumulated it will pay the price. Are these leaders evil? The question is irrelevant. The incentive structure guarantees this outcome regardless of who holds office.

> "Absolute power corrupts absolutely."
>
> \- Lord Acton, 1887

Hanlon's razor puts the point plainly: never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity. The debt is the work of a structure that rewards borrowing, and the structure will keep producing it no matter who holds office.

## **The honest man loses**

One might object that a truly moral person would resist these incentives. The objection fails on the arithmetic of competition.

A person who is perfectly moral and forced to go against every incentive given to them is eventually tired out. The honest politician who refuses to exploit the system gets outcompeted, primaried, defunded, and replaced by someone willing to play the game. In a competitive realm, the person who does evil wins when the incentives reward evil. If the incentive structure is bad, only those who take advantage of the incentive structure will win. This is selection pressure applied to governance. Character alone cannot fix a broken structure because the system selects for those who will exploit it.

We have set up our incentives to ensure that we, the people, lose every time.

## **The banality of silence**

Hannah Arendt covered the trial of Adolf Eichmann in 1963. She went to Jerusalem and sat through the testimony of a career-minded functionary, a man who had risen through his organization by following its incentive structure upward. Arendt called it the banality of evil.

Eichmann's job was scheduling the trains that moved people to the camps. He was good at it, and he rose through the ranks by being good at it. He barely thought about his victims at all. The incentives in front of him rewarded efficiency, obedience, and career advancement, and he followed them. The horror was that evil required only people who followed the incentives in front of them and looked away from what the paperwork actually meant.

For every bank robber there are thousands who slip a small item into a pocket at the dollar store. Most of the harm in the world happens when the easy path is the wrong one, and people take it. True evil lives in silence. It lives in the failure to stand against what is wrong when standing costs everything.

> "First they came for the Communists / And I did not speak out / Because I was not a Communist. Then they came for the Socialists / And I did not speak out / Because I was not a Socialist. Then they came for the trade unionists / And I did not speak out / Because I was not a trade unionist. Then they came for the Jews / And I did not speak out / Because I was not a Jew. Then they came for me / And there was no one left to speak for me."
>
> \- Pastor Martin Niemöller

Each group calculated that silence was cheaper than solidarity. They were right in the short term and destroyed in the long term. When incentive structures punish dissent, silence becomes the path of least resistance.

## **Governing incentives**

The current system asks citizens to choose between promises. Officials then enter a system whose every incentive contradicts those promises. The result is predictable and unchanging.

Cyber Sovereignty proposes a different architecture. People choose between working systems, and government is judged by its results. Citizens vote with their feet, selecting the society that delivers on its commitments. Systems that fail lose their citizens to systems that succeed. The governing question becomes: does this system produce good outcomes for those within it?

So next time someone says the government is evil, ask them what government officials are incentivized to do in our system. The design of incentive structures is the pinnacle consequential act a political society can undertake.
